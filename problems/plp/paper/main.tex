\title{Fast exact overlap minimization for three-dimensional irregular strip packing of polyhedra}

\author[pucv]{Ignacio Araya\corref{cor1}}
\ead{ignacio.araya@pucv.cl}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author}
\address[pucv]{Escuela de Ingenier\'ia Inform\'atica, Pontificia Universidad Cat\'olica de Valpara\'iso, Chile}

\begin{abstract}
We address the three-dimensional irregular strip packing problem: polyhedra, possibly non-convex, must be placed
by translation in a container with a fixed rectangular base so that the height is minimized. We propose an
overlap-minimization heuristic that works with the exact geometry of the pieces, represented as unions of convex
parts. Its new element is an exact axis move. The overlap of two parts is measured by the penetration depth of
their relative position in their no-fit polytope; along any axis this function is zero outside an interval and
concave inside it, so the best position of a piece along an axis is found by evaluating a few breakpoints only.
The rest of the method adapts a few known components from two-dimensional nesting: a greedy
construction, a strategic oscillation of the container height with guided local search weights, a local
improvement and restarts. On the benchmarks of polyhedral pieces, the method improves the best known heights of
11 of 13 instances within two minutes, reaches in 10 seconds the quality that the previous exact local search
attains in 250 seconds (time normalized to our hardware), and matches 24 of the 27 known optima of small
instances. An ablation study shows that the speed of the move, the guided local search weights and the effort
spent in each separation are the decisive elements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Three-dimensional irregular packing arises in additive manufacturing (packing parts in the build volume of a
printer), in the loading of non-rectangular goods, and in the design of compact assemblies. In its open-dimension
variant, the \emph{3D irregular strip packing problem}, a set of polyhedra must be placed without overlap inside
a container with a fixed $W\times D$ base, and the height of the packing is to be minimized. We consider the
translational version: pieces keep their orientation.

The literature on this problem is small compared with its two-dimensional counterpart, and it is split by the
representation of the geometry. Exact approaches use the polyhedra directly: phi-functions and nonlinear
programming \citep{stoyan2004,stoyan2005,romanova2018}, the 3DNest local search of \citet{egeblad2009}, which
minimizes the exact overlap volume along axis-parallel translations, constructive heuristics such as HAPE3D
\citep{liu2015}, and, recently, mixed-integer models based on no-fit polyhedra that solve small convex instances
to optimality \citep{tollenaere2026}. Approximate approaches discretize the pieces into voxels
\citep{lamas2023}; voxels make overlap tests trivial and scale well, but they overestimate the pieces and the
quality of the solutions depends on the resolution.

In two dimensions, the most successful methods for irregular strip packing are overlap-minimization heuristics:
the height (length) is decreased, the pieces are allowed to overlap, and a local search moves them until the
overlap vanishes \citep{egeblad2007,umetani2009,imamichi2009,elkeran2013,gardeyn2025}. The key ingredients are a
cheap and informative overlap measure, moves that can be optimized exactly or quickly, and guided local search
(GLS) weights to escape local minima. This paper brings these ingredients to three-dimensional polyhedra with an
exact representation. Most components of the method are adaptations of known ideas, whose origin is given in
Section~\ref{sec:method}. The new element is the overlap measure and the move built on it: the penetration depth
in the no-fit polytopes (NFPs) of convex parts which, unlike the overlap volume used by 3DNest, can be minimized
exactly along an axis by evaluating a few breakpoints.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A fast exact overlap-minimization move for 3D polyhedra represented as unions of convex parts. It brings
the exact axis moves of \citet{umetani2009}, defined for 2D polygons with directional penetration depths, to 3D
and non-convex pieces: the overlap is measured by the penetration depth in the NFP of each pair of convex parts,
which along an axis is concave on an interval and zero outside it, so the optimal position of a piece along an
axis is attained at an interval endpoint (Proposition~\ref{prop:axis}). With an implementation that evaluates
each term only at the breakpoints inside its interval, the move is much cheaper than the exact overlap volume of
\citet{egeblad2009}, and this speed is what makes exact geometry practical in a 3D metaheuristic.
\item A method built around this move with few components, adapted from known methods (a bottom-left
construction with gravity, a strategic oscillation of the height, multiplicative GLS weights \citep{gardeyn2025},
a compaction of the feasible layouts and restarts), and an ablation study showing which elements matter: the GLS weights, the
speed of the move and the effort spent in each separation are decisive, the compaction is needed to reach
the optima, and the perturbation of the restarts and the tuned construction criterion have no significant
effect.
\item New best known solutions on the Merged instances of \citet{egeblad2009} and on classical instances of
\citet{stoyan2004,stoyan2005}, with a time-matched comparison against the exact method 3DNest, and a comparison
with the optimal values and bounds of the mixed-integer models of \citet{tollenaere2026}.
\item An exact convex decomposition of the non-convex (prismatic) pieces of \citet{ikonen1997} used in the
Merged instances.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:problem} defines the problem and the geometric tools. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the
algorithm, and Section~\ref{sec:exp} the experiments.

\section{Problem and geometry}\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Problem}
We are given a container base of width $W>0$ and depth $D>0$, of unbounded height, and $n$ pieces
$P_1,\dots,P_n\subset\mathbb{R}^3$ (polyhedra, with multiplicities). Each piece is a union of convex polytopes,
$P_i=\bigcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}_i}A_a$, expressed in a local frame where the minimum of its bounding box is the
origin; $w_i,d_i,h_i$ denote its bounding-box dimensions. A solution is a vector of translations
$t_i=(x_i,y_i,z_i)\in\mathbb{R}^3$, $i=1,\dots,n$ (continuous positions; rotations are not allowed), such that
\begin{align}
& 0\le x_i\le W-w_i,\quad 0\le y_i\le D-d_i,\quad z_i\ge 0, && i=1,\dots,n,\\
& \operatorname{int}(P_i+t_i)\cap\operatorname{int}(P_j+t_j)=\emptyset, && i<j,
\end{align}
where $\operatorname{int}(\cdot)$ denotes the interior of a set (pieces may touch but not overlap), and the
objective is to minimize $H=\max_i(z_i+h_i)$. A trivial lower bound is
$H_0=\max\{\max_i h_i,\ \sum_i\operatorname{vol}(P_i)/(WD)\}$.

\subsection{No-fit polytopes and penetration depth}
For two convex parts $A$ and $B$ placed at $t_A$ and $t_B$, their interiors intersect if and only if the relative
position $r=t_A-t_B$ lies in the interior of the no-fit polytope
\begin{equation}
N_{AB}=B\oplus(-A)=\operatorname{conv}\{b-a:\ a\in V(A),\ b\in V(B)\},
\end{equation}
where $V(\cdot)$ is the vertex set \citep{bennell2008}. We compute $N_{AB}$ once per pair of parts as the convex
hull of the vertex differences and store it in H-representation, $N_{AB}=\{r:\ n_f\cdot r\le d_f,\ f\in F_{AB}\}$,
with unit normals $\|n_f\|=1$.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{penetration} of $A$ and $B$ at relative position $r$ is
$\delta_{AB}(r)=\max\{0,\ \min_{f\in F_{AB}}(d_f-n_f\cdot r)\}$.
\end{definition}

For $r$ inside $N_{AB}$, $\delta_{AB}(r)$ is the Euclidean distance from $r$ to the boundary of $N_{AB}$, i.e.,
the length of the shortest translation that separates $A$ and $B$, known as the translational penetration
depth \citep{cameron1986,agarwal2000}. It is
zero if and only if the parts do not overlap. For two pieces we use
$\delta_{ij}(t_i-t_j)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_i}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}_j}\delta_{ab}(t_i-t_j)$, where only pairs of parts
whose bounding boxes intersect are evaluated. Figure~\ref{fig:nfp} illustrates both definitions in two dimensions.

\subsection{Exact minimization along an axis}
Let all pieces but $i$ be fixed, and let $i$ move along axis $e_k$, $t_i(s)=t_i+(s-t_{ik})e_k$, with
$s\in[0,U_k]$ (the container limits, using the current height $H$ for the vertical axis). The weighted overlap
of $i$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:obj}
\phi(s)=\sum_{j\ne i}w_{ij}\,\delta_{ij}\big(t_i(s)-t_j\big),
\end{equation}
where the weights $w_{ij}\ge 1$ (initially 1) are the penalties of guided local search (GLS)
\citep{voudouris1999}: they are increased for the pairs that remain overlapping at local minima, so that the
search focuses on them (Section~\ref{sec:minover}).

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:axis}
Each term $\delta_{ab}(t_i(s)-t_j)$ is zero outside an interval $(L_{ab},U_{ab})$ and concave inside it. Hence
$\phi$ is concave on every interval between consecutive points of
$\mathcal{B}=\{0,U_k\}\cup\{L_{ab},U_{ab}\}$, and a minimizer of $\phi$ on $[0,U_k]$ is attained at a point of
$\mathcal{B}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Along the line, $d_f-n_f\cdot r(s)=c_f-n_{fk}s$ is affine. The set where all of them are positive is the open
interval $(L_{ab},U_{ab})$ with $L_{ab}=\max_{n_{fk}<0}c_f/n_{fk}$ and $U_{ab}=\min_{n_{fk}>0}c_f/n_{fk}$
(facets with $n_{fk}=0$ only decide whether the interval is empty). Inside it, $\delta_{ab}$ is a minimum of
affine functions, hence concave; outside it is zero. Between consecutive points of $\mathcal{B}$ every term is
either concave or identically zero, so $\phi$ is concave there and attains its minimum at an endpoint.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:axis} is the counterpart, for the Euclidean penetration depth of convex parts in 3D, of
the piecewise linear directional penetration depths of \citet{umetani2009} in 2D, which require a trapezoidal
decomposition of each NFP and have no practical equivalent for 3D NFPs. With pieces decomposed into convex parts,
every NFP is convex and each term reduces to a minimum of affine functions. Figure~\ref{fig:axis} illustrates the
proposition in two dimensions.

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/axis.pdf}
\caption{Proposition~\ref{prop:axis} in 2D, with the parts of Figure~\ref{fig:nfp}. (a) Part $A$ moves along
$x$ with $B$ fixed: it touches $B$ at $s=L$ and $s=U$ (dashed) and overlaps it in between (red). (b) In the space of
relative positions, $r(s)$ moves along a line that enters $N_{AB}$ at $L$ and leaves it at $U$. (c) For each
facet (same colors as in (b)), $d_f-n_f\cdot r(s)$ is affine in $s$; the penetration is the lower envelope of these

minimum (star, solid outline in (d)) is at a breakpoint, and only these points are evaluated.}
\label{fig:axis}
\end{figure}

The move evaluates $\phi$ at the points of $\mathcal{B}$ only. With the interval of each term known, a term is
evaluated only at the candidates inside it: sorting the candidates, each term contributes to a contiguous range
found by binary search. Facets are stored as $(c_f,n_{fk})$ in contiguous arrays, so evaluating a term costs one
fused multiply-add per facet. Ties are broken towards the smaller coordinate (gravity and walls).

\section{Algorithm}\label{sec:method}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:main} summarizes the method. A greedy construction gives a first feasible solution $S$ of
height $H$. The search then tries to fit the pieces in a slightly lower container of height $h<H$: the positions
of $S$ are \emph{clamped} to $h$, which creates overlaps, and \textsc{MinimizeOverlap} moves the pieces to remove
them. Each call of \textsc{MinimizeOverlap} is a \emph{separation attempt}. It \emph{succeeds} if it reaches a
layout without overlap, which then fits in height $h$; it \emph{fails} if overlap remains after its maximum number
of passes. After a success, the layout is compacted and becomes the new best solution, and the target is lowered
again. After a failure, the target is raised slightly and the attempt continues from the overlapping layout; when
the target reaches $H$, the search restarts from the best solution. The components are described in the order
in which they are needed: the construction (Section~\ref{sec:greedy}), the overlap minimization
(Section~\ref{sec:minover}), the oscillation of the target height and the restarts (Section~\ref{sec:osc}), and
the compaction of the feasible layouts (Section~\ref{sec:improve}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Overview}\label{alg:main}
\SetCommentSty{textit}
$S\gets$ \textsc{Compact}(\textsc{Greedy}()); $H\gets H(S)$\;
$h\gets(1-\rho)H$; $T\gets$ \textsc{Clamp}($S,h$); $w\gets 1$\tcp*{overlaps appear}
\While{time remains}{
  \eIf(\tcp*[f]{success: $T$ is feasible}){\textsc{MinimizeOverlap}($T,h,w$)}{
    $S\gets$ \textsc{Compact}($T$); $H\gets H(S)$\tcp*{new best solution, $H\le h$}
    $h\gets(1-\rho)H$; $T\gets$ \textsc{Clamp}($S,h$); $w\gets 1$\;
  }(\tcp*[f]{failure}){
    $h\gets h+\rho H/4$; $T\gets$ \textsc{Clamp}($T,h$)\tcp*{keeps the overlaps and $w$}
    \If{$h\ge H$}{
      $h\gets(1-\rho)H$; $T\gets$ \textsc{Clamp}($S,h$); move a random piece of $T$; $w\gets 1$\tcp*{restart}
    }
  }
}
\Return $S$\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Greedy construction}\label{sec:greedy}
Pieces are placed one at a time on top of the current load (Algorithm~\ref{alg:greedy}). For each type of piece
still to be placed, $g\times g$ starting points ($g=4$) are taken on a regular grid of the base, at the current
height $H_{\text{cur}}$ of the load, and from each of them the piece descends (\textsc{Descend}, lines~9--18).
The descent is a sequence of classical linear programs (LPs) in the three translation variables $u=(x,y,z)$ of the
piece. At the current point $t$, for every placed part $b$ that the part $a$ of the piece may meet within a trust
region (a box of half side $\Delta$, the largest dimension of the piece), one facet $n_f\cdot r\ge d_f$ of $N_{ab}$
satisfied at $t$ is selected: the one that allows the longest step in the descent direction (line~13). Since
$N_{ab}$ is convex, the half-space beyond any of its facets contains no overlapping position, so the selected
facets define a convex region of positions where the piece does not overlap the placed parts. The LP of line~14
is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:descendlp}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{u}\ \ & c\cdot u\\
\text{s.t.}\ \ & n_f\cdot(u-t_b)\ge d_f\quad\text{for every selected facet $f$ of $N_{ab}$},\\
& \max(\ell,\,t-\Delta)\le u\le\min(\bar u,\,t+\Delta),\quad u_z\le t_z,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $t_b$ is the position of the piece of $b$ and $[\ell,\bar u]$ are the positions that keep the piece inside the
container. The objective $c=(\pm\sigma,\pm\sigma,1)$ moves the piece down and, with a small weight $\sigma$, towards
the walls in $x$ and $y$ nearest to the starting point (the signs are fixed at the start, so the objective is linear).
The current point $t$ is feasible, and every feasible point is a position without overlap, so each LP can only
improve the position. With three variables and a few dozen constraints, the LP is solved by a small primal
active-set method (a simplex method in the space of $u$) that starts at $t$ and keeps every iterate feasible; no
external solver is needed. The facets are re-selected at the new point and the procedure stops when the
objective no longer decreases. Figure~\ref{fig:descend} shows the LPs of one descent. \textsc{Descend}
combines two classical ideas: bottom-left placement rules for irregular pieces \citep{jakobs1996}, here with
priority to gravity, as the minimum potential energy principle of HAPE3D \citep{liu2015}, and the compaction of a
layout by linear programs over NFP facets \citep{li1995,gomes2006}, here applied to a single piece.

Among all piece types and resulting positions, the greedy places the pair $(k,t)$ that maximizes (line~6)
\begin{equation}\label{eq:greedy}
\begin{aligned}
\mathit{val}(k,t)={}&\alpha\log\frac{V_k}{V_{\max}}-\beta\,\frac{z}{H_0}\\
&-\gamma\,\frac{\max(0,\,z+h_k-H_{\text{cur}})}{H_0},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $V_k$ is the volume of the piece, $V_{\max}$ the largest volume, $z$ the height of the position, $h_k$ the
height of the piece and $H_{\text{cur}}$ the current height of the load. The first term favors large pieces, as the classical orderings by
decreasing volume, but on a logarithmic scale, so that a smaller piece is preferred when it fits clearly lower.
The second term favors low positions, i.e., filling the gaps of the load before building on top of it, and the
third penalizes positions that raise the height of the load. Normalizing by the lower bound $H_0$ makes the
parameters independent of the scale of the instance. For a given piece the score decreases with $z$, so the
lowest of its positions is always preferred; the score thus decides which piece is placed next: a large piece
if it fits in a low gap, and a smaller one if the large pieces would raise the height (the values of the
parameters are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:exp}). Figure~\ref{fig:construction} illustrates the construction
in two dimensions.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
\caption{Greedy construction}\label{alg:greedy}
\SetCommentSty{textit}
\Fn{\textsc{Greedy}()}{
  $\mathcal{L}\gets\emptyset$ (placed pieces); $H_{\text{cur}}\gets 0$\;
  \While{some piece is not placed}{
    \ForEach{type $k$ with pieces left \KwSty{and} each of $g\times g$ grid points $(x,y)$ of the base}{
      $t\gets$ \textsc{Descend}($k,(x,y,H_{\text{cur}}),\mathcal{L}$)\;
      $\mathit{val}\gets\mathit{val}(k,t)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:greedy}\;
    }
    place the pair $(k,t)$ of largest $\mathit{val}$ in $\mathcal{L}$; $H_{\text{cur}}\gets\max(H_{\text{cur}},z+h_k)$\;
  }
  \Return $\mathcal{L}$\;
}
\Fn{\textsc{Descend}($k,t,\mathcal{L}$)}{
  $c\gets(\pm\sigma,\pm\sigma,1)$\tcp*{down and towards the nearest walls}
  \Repeat{no improvement}{
    \ForEach{part $a$ of $k$ and placed part $b$ of $\mathcal{L}$ that may meet $a$ in the trust region}{
      select the facet $f$ of $N_{ab}$ satisfied at $t$ that allows the longest step along $-c$\;
    }
    $t'\gets$ optimal solution of the LP~\eqref{eq:descendlp} built at $t$\;
    \lIf{$c\cdot t'\ge c\cdot t$}{\Break}
    $t\gets t'$\;
  }
  \Return $t$\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Overlap minimization}\label{sec:minover}
Given a layout $T$ with overlaps in a container of height $h$, \textsc{MinimizeOverlap} minimizes the total
overlap $\sum_{i<j}\delta_{ij}$ by moving one piece at a time along an axis. Moving one piece at a time along the
axes to the position of minimum overlap is the neighborhood of \citet{egeblad2007,egeblad2009}, who minimize the
overlap area or volume; \citet{umetani2009} and \citet{imamichi2009} measure the overlap of 2D polygons by the
penetration depth in the NFP, which we use for 3D unions of convex parts.

Such a local search soon reaches local minima, layouts in which no single move reduces the overlap. To escape
them we use guided local search (GLS) \citep{voudouris1999}, as \citet{egeblad2007} and \citet{umetani2009} do
for nesting. GLS changes the objective instead of the layout: each pair of pieces has a weight $w_{ij}\ge1$, the
moves minimize the weighted overlap $\phi=\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}\,\delta_{ij}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:obj}, and at a local
minimum the weights of the pairs that still overlap are increased. A persistent overlap thus becomes more
expensive, and the next moves remove it even at the cost of creating smaller overlaps elsewhere, which in turn
gain weight if they persist. We update the weights multiplicatively as in \citet{gardeyn2025}: at each local
minimum, the weight of every overlapping pair is multiplied by $1.2+0.8\,\delta_{ij}/\max\delta$, and the weights
of the other pairs decay by a factor $0.95$ (minimum 1). All the weights start at 1. Each element of this rule has
a simple purpose:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Multiplicative growth.} The weight of a pair that keeps overlapping grows geometrically: after five
consecutive local minima it is between $1.2^5\approx2.5$ and $2^5=32$ times its initial value. A persistent overlap
therefore soon dominates $\phi$, however many other pairs there are, and the search is forced to remove it. With
additive weights ($w_{ij}\gets w_{ij}+1$) the growth is linear, so the relative pressure on a persistent pair
weakens as the weights of all pairs accumulate.
\item \emph{Factor proportional to the penetration.} The factor ranges from 1.2, for the shallowest overlap, to 2,
for the deepest one. Shallow overlaps are often removed by the next moves without help, while deep ones need a
large change of the layout, so the deepest overlaps are penalized fastest.
\item \emph{Decay.} The weight of a pair that no longer overlaps returns slowly to 1. The weights thus reflect the
recent history of the search: a pair that overlapped long ago does not keep constraining the moves, and its
weight starts growing from a low value if it overlaps again.
\end{itemize}
The ablation of Section~\ref{sec:exp} confirms that this rule matters: additive weights increase the heights by
1.4\% (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

In Algorithm~\ref{alg:minover}, line~2 computes the penetrations of all pairs, and each iteration of the loop is
a pass. Only the pieces involved in an overlapping pair are moved (line~7), in random order, each along the three
axes in random order with the exact move of Proposition~\ref{prop:axis} (line~9): $\phi$ is evaluated at the
breakpoints and the best one is taken; since the current position is a candidate, a move never increases $\phi$.
After moving a piece, only its row of the penetration matrix is recomputed (line~11). A pass without gain
(line~12) is a local minimum of $\phi$, and the weights are updated. The procedure succeeds when no overlap
remains (then verified with a separating-axis test) and fails after 500 passes. Figure~\ref{fig:passes} shows all
the passes on the 2D example, from a clamped layout.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
\caption{Overlap minimization}\label{alg:minover}
\SetCommentSty{textit}
\Fn{\textsc{MinimizeOverlap}($T,h,w$)}{
  $P_{ij}\gets\delta_{ij}(t_i-t_j)$ for all pairs\tcp*{penetration matrix}
  \For{$\mathit{pass}=1,\dots,500$}{
    $O\gets\{(i,j):P_{ij}>0\}$\;
    \lIf{$O=\emptyset$}{\Return \KwSty{true} if the separating-axis test confirms it}
    $\mathit{gain}\gets 0$\;
    \ForEach{piece $i$ of a pair of $O$, in random order}{
      \ForEach{axis $k\in\{x,y,z\}$ in random order}{
        $s^*\gets\arg\min_{s\in\mathcal{B}}\phi(s)$ over the breakpoints $\mathcal{B}$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:axis},
        with the container $[0,W-w_i]\times[0,D-d_i]\times[0,h-h_i]$\;
        $\mathit{gain}\gets\mathit{gain}+\phi(t_{ik})-\phi(s^*)$; $t_{ik}\gets s^*$\;
      }
      recompute row $i$ of $P$\;
    }
    \If(\tcp*[f]{local minimum: GLS}){$\mathit{gain}=0$}{
      $w_{ij}\gets\max(1,\,0.95\,w_{ij})$ for $(i,j)\notin O$;\quad
      $w_{ij}\gets w_{ij}\,(1.2+0.8\,P_{ij}/\max_O P)$ for $(i,j)\in O$\;
    }
  }
  \Return \KwSty{false}\;
}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Strategic oscillation and restarts}\label{sec:osc}
The target height $h$ is set from the best height $H$ and clamped layouts,
\begin{equation}
\textsc{Clamp}(T,h):\quad z_i\gets\min\{z_i,\ h-h_i\},\qquad i=1,\dots,n,
\end{equation}
which moves every piece that exceeds $h$ down until its top is at $h$, regardless of the other pieces, and thus
creates overlaps among the pieces near the top. After a success, the target is lowered to $(1-\rho)H$, with
$\rho=1\%$, below the new best height, the best solution is clamped to it and the weights are reset. After a
failure, the target is raised by $\rho H/4$ and the next attempt starts from the overlapping layout left by the
failed one, with its weights, so that a few failures are allowed before giving up the current layout. This scheme
of shrinking the container, allowing overlap and separating the pieces comes from the 2D methods of
\citet{egeblad2007}, \citet{umetani2009} and \citet{imamichi2009}, and was used in 3D by \citet{egeblad2009}; the
clamp is the counterpart of moving the pieces that exceed the new strip length inside it.

When the target reaches the best height again, the search restarts from the best solution clamped to
$(1-\rho)H$, with the weights reset, after moving one random piece (a kick) to a random position in the
container. This is the perturbation step of iterated local search \citep{lourenco2003}, which
\citet{imamichi2009} applied to nesting. The kick matters on small instances: with two pieces there is a single
pair, so the weights have no effect, and without it the search keeps returning to the layout produced by the
greedy order.

\subsection{Compaction of feasible layouts}\label{sec:improve}
\textsc{MinimizeOverlap} stops as soon as no overlap remains: the layout fits in the target height, but its
pieces are where the last moves left them, often with free space below them, since a move along $z$ goes to the
position of least overlap, not to the lowest one. Without a further step, the height of the best solution would
decrease only in the discrete steps $\rho$ of the oscillation. \textsc{Compact} lets the pieces settle onto each
other, in the sense of the compaction of \citet{li1995}. It is applied to every feasible layout: after each
successful separation and after the construction, where it rarely changes anything, since the greedy already
leaves each piece at the end of a descent.

A pass of \textsc{Compact} takes the pieces in increasing order of $z$; each one descends again with
\textsc{Descend}, with the other pieces fixed, and if it defines the height, it is moved to the lowest of the
positions generated as in the greedy construction, if that is lower. Passes are repeated while the height
decreases (at most 50). A pass can enable moves that were not possible in the previous one, because the
constraints of \textsc{Descend}, the NFP facets of the neighboring pieces, change when those pieces move, and with
irregular pieces the order by the bottom is not the order in which they support each other. For example, a piece
$A$ may lean on the inclined side of a wedge $B$ whose narrow bottom is higher than the bottom of $A$: $A$ is
processed first and cannot move, then $B$ descends into a gap, and only in the next pass can $A$ follow it.
Likewise, once the pieces that defined the height have moved lower, other pieces define it and are tried in the
next pass. In a run on Stoyan 2004 Example 2, after a separation at height 40.19, the successive passes lowered
the height to 39.80, 38.71 and 38.24, by descents alone: most of the gain came after the first pass. With
\textsc{Compact}, the optima of the small instances are reached exactly (Section~\ref{sec:t2025}).

\section{Computational experiments}\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Setting}
The method is implemented in C++ and needs no external solver. Experiments ran on two Intel Xeon E5-2620 v4 processors (2.1~GHz, 3.0~GHz turbo, 16 cores, 64~GB, Ubuntu 24.04,
g++ 13.3), one thread per run, 14 runs in parallel. Each configuration was run with 10 seeds and a time limit of 120 s per
instance. All reported solutions were verified with an independent checker that tests every pair of convex parts
for a separating axis, with a tolerance of $10^{-6}$ on the penetration depth (the containers are 10 to 60 units
wide). Table~\ref{tab:params} lists the parameters of the method. Those of the construction were
tuned with Optuna \citep{akiba2019} on 16 training instances, with an earlier version of the method; the weight update follows
\citet{gardeyn2025}; the step $\rho$ and the number of passes are analyzed in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.

\paragraph{Instances}
We use the 3D instances of the ESICUP repository \citep{esicup}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Stoyan 2005}, Examples 1--3 (7, 12 and 25 convex pieces) \citep{stoyan2005}. In the public data, one
piece (polytope7) has faces that use only 9 of its 10 vertices; we use its convex hull (volume 192.5), which
matches the volume reported by \citet{egeblad2009}.
\item \emph{Stoyan 2004}, Examples 1--5 (20--50 non-convex pieces) \citep{stoyan2004}, each defined as a union of
convex polytopes, which we use directly as the decomposition.
\item \emph{Merged1--5} \citep{egeblad2009}: $i$ copies of 15 pieces, the 7 convex pieces of Stoyan 2005 and the
8 pieces of \citet{ikonen1997} scaled by 4, in a container whose base is such that a layout with 50\%
utilization is a cube. Six Ikonen pieces are non-convex prisms; we decompose them exactly by triangulating the
base polygon, merging triangles into convex polygons \citep{hertel1983}, and extruding (a frame is split into
four boxes; two pieces have polygons touching at a vertex). The volumes of the parts match those of the meshes.
\item \emph{Liu Experiment 1} \citep{liu2015}: 36 pieces (8 tetrahedra, 8 cubes, 8 stars, 8 arrows and 4 rings)
in a $20\times20$ container; the stars, arrows and rings are prisms and are decomposed exactly in the same way.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{References}
3DNest \citep{egeblad2009} uses the same exact geometry; its average and maximum utilization over 10 runs of
600 s (one core of a 2.16~GHz Core Duo) are converted to heights. \citet{lamas2023} report best and average heights
of a voxel VNS; their voxelization overestimates the pieces (by 15\% in volume for Merged1, as they
note), so their solutions are feasible for the original pieces. For Stoyan 2004 the only exact reference is \citet{stoyan2004}.

\subsection{Component analysis}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} evaluates the components on the seven hardest instances (Stoyan 2004 Examples 1--5 and
Stoyan 2005 Examples 2--3). Each row changes one element of the method:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Without \textsc{Compact}}: no compaction after the construction and the separations
(Section~\ref{sec:improve}).
\item \emph{$\sigma=0$}: \textsc{Descend} without the wall term (gravity only).
\item \emph{Greedy by volume}: the largest piece first, at its lowest position ($\alpha$ large, $\gamma=0$), instead
of the tuned criterion~\eqref{eq:greedy}.
\item \emph{No kick}: the restarts do not move any piece.
\item \emph{Additive weights}: $w_{ij}\gets w_{ij}+1$ for the overlapping pairs at local minima.
\item \emph{Time limit of 24 s}: the same runs stopped at 24 s instead of 120 s, i.e., with a fifth of the time.
\item \emph{Sensitivity}: step $\rho$ of the oscillation and maximum number of passes of
\textsc{MinimizeOverlap}.
\end{itemize}
For each variant, the last column of the table gives the mean relative change of the height with respect to the
proposed method over the 70 runs (7 instances, 10 seeds), positive when the heights are higher (worse), and the
$p$-value of a Wilcoxon signed-rank test that pairs the runs by instance and seed.

The decisive elements are the GLS weights and the effort spent in each separation. Additive weights increase
the heights by 1.4\% ($p<0.001$). With a fifth of the time (24 s), the heights increase by 2.3\%
($p<0.001$) and most of the advantage over the published values is lost (the gap on Stoyan 2004 drops from
$-2.9\%$ to $-0.1\%$): the method needs many moves, so their speed matters as much as their exactness. The number of passes of
\textsc{MinimizeOverlap} also matters: with 100 passes the heights increase by 1.8\% ($p<0.001$), since
separations that would succeed are abandoned, and with 2000 passes by 0.7\% ($p=0.008$), since the time is spent
on hopeless ones. The compaction is needed: without it the heights increase by 0.4\% ($p=0.10$) and, in a
separate run of all the instances, only 19 instead of 24 of the 27 optima of Section~\ref{sec:t2025} are found,
because the height of the best solution then decreases only in the discrete steps of the oscillation. The other
elements have no significant effect ($p\ge0.06$): the wall term ($\sigma=0$: $+0.5\%$), the tuned greedy criterion
(greedy by volume: $+0.3\%$), the step $\rho$, and the kick (no kick: $+0.2\%$). On these instances the
diversification thus comes from the restart itself, with reset weights and a new random order of the moves; the
random move is needed only on small instances (Section~\ref{sec:t2025}).

\subsection{Time-matched comparison}
Hardware differs by about ten years: 3DNest ran on one core of a 2.16~GHz Intel Core Duo (2006), and the
single-thread ratings of PassMark \citep{passmark} are 667 for that processor (Core Duo T2600) and 1624 for ours
(Xeon E5-2620 v4), a factor of about 2.4 per core; this is rather an upper bound, since the rating uses the maximum
turbo frequency and our runs share the processor. Figure~\ref{fig:time} compares the mean utilization over time with that
of 3DNest at 10, 60, 300 and 600 seconds, converted to our machine. Our first solution is available after about 0.3 seconds (the
greedy construction), and at 10 seconds our mean utilization already exceeds the mean utilization
of 3DNest at 600 seconds on all five instances (44.1, 43.1, 44.9, 43.8 and 43.1\% versus 43.0, 42.9, 43.3, 42.0
and 41.0\%). With the factor 2.4, our 10 seconds correspond to about 24 seconds on the machine of
\citet{egeblad2009}, and their 600 seconds to about 250 seconds on ours, so the advantage holds at equal
effective time by more than an order of magnitude.

\subsection{Discussion}
The results support a conclusion that agrees with recent 2D work \citep{gardeyn2025}: the quality of an
overlap-minimization heuristic is driven by how many separation attempts fit in the time budget, and therefore
by the cost of evaluating and optimizing the overlap. The NFP-based penetration depth gives an exact, cheap and
informative measure for unions of convex parts with few facets, which is the case of all the benchmark
instances.

\paragraph{Realistic meshes}
The cost of a move grows with the number of facets of the NFPs, so pieces with curved surfaces are the hard case.
We tested the Chess instance of \citet{lamas2023} (32 pieces, meshes of 700--2900 triangles) with conservative
convex decompositions computed with CoACD \citep{wei2022}, whose union contains each piece, so the solutions are
feasible for the real geometry. The NFPs between parts then have hundreds of facets, and a separation takes
20--35 s instead of a fraction of a second. Combining a coarse decomposition (1--4 parts per piece, 15 min) with a
finer one (6--20 parts, 5 min), the method reaches a mean height of 148.4 (best 138.6), against 135.1 (best 131)
for the voxel VNS of \citet{lamas2023} (Table~\ref{tab:results}), whose cost does not depend on the complexity of
the surface. Voxels and the proposed method are thus complementary: the latter is faster and exact on polyhedral
pieces with few facets, the former scales better to curved meshes.

\paragraph{Limitations}
Rotations are not considered. The method requires a convex decomposition of each piece; for the benchmark
instances it is exact and small, while for curved meshes a conservative decomposition overestimates the pieces
and, above all, makes the moves expensive. Reducing the cost of the NFPs of curved parts (e.g., hierarchical
filtering with the hull of each piece, or conservative simplification of the NFPs) is left for future work.

\section{Conclusions}
We presented an overlap-minimization heuristic for 3D irregular strip packing that works with the exact geometry
of polyhedra given as unions of convex parts. Its key element is an exact axis move based on penetration depths
in no-fit polytopes, combined with multiplicative guided local search, strategic oscillation and restarts. This simple method outperforms
the previous methods, with exact and voxel geometry, on the benchmarks of polyhedral pieces, and matches almost
all the proven optima of the small instances. On curved meshes, which require conservative decompositions with
many facets, it remains behind voxel-based search. Future work includes cheaper NFP computations for curved
parts, rotations, larger instances from additive manufacturing, and lower bounds.
