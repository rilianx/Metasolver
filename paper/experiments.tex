\section{Computational experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

This section evaluates the proposed algorithm on the benchmark of
\citet{Paquay2018b} and on the two adapted variants introduced by \citet{Hessler2025}, and
compares it instance by instance with the randomized greedy search (RGS) of
\citet{Hessler2025}, which, to the best of our knowledge, reports the best results on these
instances. Section~\ref{sec:exp-setup} describes the instances, the implementation, the
parameter tuning and the evaluation protocol. Section~\ref{sec:exp-results} reports the main
results, Section~\ref{sec:exp-bound} compares them with a lower bound,
Section~\ref{sec:exp-time} analyzes the running time, Section~\ref{sec:exp-ablation} studies
the contribution of each component and Section~\ref{sec:exp-tradeoff} the trade-off between the
number of ULDs and the weight distribution.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

\paragraph{Instances.}
We use the 300 final instances of \citet{Paquay2018b}: ten groups of 30 instances with
$|I| \in \{10, 20, \dots, 100\}$ boxes sampled from a real set of 562 boxes, highly heterogeneous,
with weights, allowed vertical orientations and fragile (non-stackable) boxes. Six ULD types
of a Boeing 777 are available in unlimited number: three lower-deck containers (types 1--3,
with up to two bottom corners cut to fit the fuselage) and three main-deck pallets and
containers (types 4--6, with up to two top corners cut). Each ULD type has a weight capacity and
a maximum deviation of the center of gravity (CoG) from its geometric center in the length and
width directions, and a maximum height of the CoG \citep{Paquay2016}.
Following \citet{Hessler2025}, we consider three settings:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Paquay}: the original instances. A box is supported if its four bottom corners
	rest on non-fragile boxes or on the floor, there is no padding material, and the allowed
	CoG deviations are those of the instance file.
\item \textbf{Adapted}: the ULD floor has an edge of width $e = 10$\,mm for the cargo net locks,
	on which no box can be loaded below the height $\delta = 10$\,mm; a substructure of height
	$\delta$ can be used to raise the floor. A box is supported if its four bottom corners are
	supported or if part of its base is directly supported and at least 90\% of its base is
	supported directly or by padding material of height at most 10\,mm. The allowed CoG
	deviation is 10\% of the half length and half width of the ULD. These are the default
	problem parameters of \citet[Table~2]{Hessler2025}.
\item \textbf{One}: the adapted instances with only one ULD of each type, so that some boxes may
	remain unloaded. Solutions are compared lexicographically: first the number of loaded boxes,
	then the number of ULDs.
\end{itemize}
In the first two settings all the boxes must be loaded and the objective is to minimize the
number of ULDs.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
The algorithm is implemented in C++ (compiled with g++ 13.3, \texttt{-O3}). Each run uses a
single thread; the experiments were carried out on a server with two Intel Xeon E5-2620 v4
processors (8 cores each, 2.1\,GHz, 3.0\,GHz in turbo mode, hyper-threading disabled) and
62\,GB of RAM, running 14 instances in parallel.
According to the single-thread rating of PassMark \citep{PassMark}, one core of the Apple M2 Pro
used by \citet{Hessler2025} is about 2.5 times faster than one core of our processor (4105
against 1624).
The time limit $t$ is the search time per ULD (excluding block generation); we report results
for $t = 0.1$\,s and $t = 1$\,s. The code, the instances, the solutions and the scripts that
produce all the tables are available at \todo{URL del repositorio}.

\paragraph{Parameter tuning.}
The parameters of the algorithm were tuned once with irace \citep{LopezIbanez2016} on a
separate training set and are used unchanged in the three settings and for both time limits.
The training set consists of 150 preliminary instances of \citet{Paquay2018b} (30 instances
with 30, 40, 50, 60 and 100 boxes), disjoint from the final ones, in the Paquay setting with
$t = 0.1$\,s and a budget of 3000 runs. The cost of a run is the number of ULDs plus five times
the number of ULDs whose CoG is out of range, plus $(1 - u)/2$, where $u$ is the total
utilization, to break ties in favour of smaller ULDs. Table~\ref{tab:params} lists the tuned
parameters.

\paragraph{Validation and comparison.}
Every solution is written to a file (position and orientation of each box) and checked by an
independent validator, which does not share code with the solver: each box is loaded at most
once, in an allowed orientation, inside its ULD and its cuts, outside the floor edge below
$\delta$, without overlapping other boxes, supported according to the setting and not on top
of a fragile box, and the weight capacity of each ULD is respected. The validator also counts
the ULDs whose CoG is out of the allowed deviation in the length or width direction (CoG$_{xy}$)
and above the maximum height (CoG$_z$, a constraint of \citet{Paquay2016} that
\citet{Hessler2025} do not consider). All the 9000 solutions reported in this section are
feasible.
The comparison with \citet{Hessler2025} uses their instance-by-instance results (Tables 8, 10
and 12 of their online appendix). For each instance we count whether our solution is better
(more boxes loaded or, with the same boxes, fewer ULDs), equal or worse, and we test the
difference in the number of ULDs with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Each configuration is
run five times; we report the mean and the standard deviation.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:exp-results}

Table~\ref{tab:results} summarizes the results. In the Paquay setting, our algorithm uses
617.0 ULDs on average with $t = 0.1$\,s and 597.8 with $t = 1$\,s, against 708 for the RGS of
\citet{Hessler2025} and 1010 for the two-phase heuristic of \citet{Paquay2018b}, a reduction of
12.9\% and 15.6\% with respect to the RGS. It is better in 91 and 111 instances, respectively,
and worse in at most one. In the adapted setting, it uses 598.0 and 576.0 ULDs against 653
(8.4\% and 11.8\% fewer), and it is worse in at most one instance. In both settings the
differences are highly significant (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, $p < 10^{-12}$). Moreover,
almost no ULD violates the CoG constraints (at most two in 300 instances), whereas 11.2\%
(Paquay) and 7.2\% (adapted) of the ULDs of the RGS have the CoG out of range.

In the setting with one ULD of each type, our algorithm uses 706.0 and 675.8 ULDs against 729
and leaves 22 and 21 boxes unloaded (out of 16\,500) against 24, again with almost no CoG
violation (3 ULDs) against 10.1\% of the ULDs of the RGS. Instance by instance, it is better in
42 and 64 instances and worse in 22 and 20 ($p = 0.004$ and $p = 2 \cdot 10^{-8}$ for the number
of ULDs). This setting is the closest one between the methods: with a limited number of ULDs,
loading the boxes that only fit in a few ULD types matters more than the packing density, and
the RGS gives the same importance to the weight balance and to the volume utilization
($I_w = 0.5$), which allows it to load boxes at the price of violating the CoG constraint
(Section~\ref{sec:exp-tradeoff}).

The results are almost deterministic: the five runs of each configuration differ by at most
0.5 ULDs on average, because in most ULDs the search ends before the time limit.

\subsection{Comparison with a lower bound}
\label{sec:exp-bound}

A simple lower bound on the number of ULDs is the maximum of the total volume of the boxes
divided by the volume of the largest ULD, the total weight divided by the largest weight
capacity, and the size of a clique of pairwise incompatible boxes (two boxes are incompatible
if they cannot be placed together in the bounding box of any ULD type). In these instances the
bound is always given by the volume. Our solutions reach the bound, and are therefore optimal,
in 140 ($t = 0.1$\,s) and 157 ($t = 1$\,s) of the 300 instances of the Paquay setting, against
80 for the RGS. In the remaining instances our solutions use at most two ULDs more than the
bound, but the bound is weak: it assumes that the boxes fill the largest ULD completely,
whereas the non-final ULDs of our solutions are about 70\% full.

\subsection{Running time}
\label{sec:exp-time}

\citet{Hessler2025} solve each instance in 0.19\,s on average (at most 0.5\,s) in the Paquay
setting, on an Apple M2 Pro. Figure~\ref{fig:time} shows the number of ULDs obtained by our
algorithm as a function of the mean time per instance, for time limits per ULD between 0.02\,s
and 2\,s. With $t = 0.02$\,s (0.16\,s per instance on average, at most 0.52\,s) our algorithm
uses 638 ULDs, 9.9\% fewer than the RGS, and it is worse in only one instance. Increasing the
time limit keeps reducing the number of ULDs: 621 ULDs in 0.34\,s, 617 in 0.62\,s, 602 in
2.8\,s and 596 in 10.5\,s. Scaling the time of the RGS by the single-thread speed ratio of
the machines (2.5), the RGS would need about 0.48\,s per instance on our machine, between the
mean times of our algorithm with $t = 0.05$\,s (0.34\,s, 621 ULDs) and $t = 0.1$\,s (0.62\,s,
617 ULDs): at a similar running time, our algorithm uses 12--13\% fewer ULDs. This scaling is
only indicative, since the two processors have different architectures.

\subsection{Contribution of the components}
\label{sec:exp-ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the results when one component of the algorithm is removed
($t = 0.1$\,s, one run). The three components that handle the CoG are essential for the weight
distribution: without the penalty in the value of the solutions, the number of ULDs decreases
by 2--27 but 193--353 ULDs have the CoG out of range; without the translation of the load the
CoG is out of range in 84--158 ULDs, and without the grouped reload in 20--31 ULDs, which also
increases the number of ULDs (by 5--20) and, with one ULD of each type, the number of unloaded
boxes (from 22 to 39). The penalty of the CoG in the evaluation of the blocks saves 7--9 ULDs
without increasing the violations, the elimination of ULDs saves 1--13 ULDs, and the
substructure saves 20--25 ULDs in the adapted settings. Removing all of them except the
penalty and the translation increases the number of ULDs by 13 (Paquay), 36 (adapted) and 98
(one).

\subsection{Number of ULDs versus weight distribution}
\label{sec:exp-tradeoff}

The two methods treat the CoG differently: \citet{Hessler2025} combine the volume utilization
and the weight balance in the score of a solution with weights $1 - I_w$ and $I_w$, with
$I_w = 0.5$ in the adapted settings, whereas our penalty $\lambda$ makes the CoG almost a hard
constraint. As the ablation shows, relaxing it reduces the number of ULDs and of unloaded boxes
at the price of many CoG violations. To compare the methods independently of this choice,
Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff} shows the number of ULDs against the percentage of ULDs with the CoG
out of range for $\lambda \in \{0, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 12.66, 30\}$ ($t = 0.1$\,s).
In the Paquay and adapted settings the number of ULDs is almost insensitive to $\lambda$ as
long as $\lambda \geq 0.5$ (617--620 and 593--599 ULDs, with at most 9 ULDs out of range); only
$\lambda = 0$, which ignores the CoG in the value of the solutions, saves a few more ULDs (616
and 591) with 31\% and 49\% of the ULDs out of range. In both settings every value
$\lambda \geq 0.5$ dominates the RGS, which uses more ULDs and has more violations.
In the setting with one ULD of each type, every value $\lambda \geq 5$, including the tuned
one, dominates the RGS in the three criteria (705--710 ULDs, 22--23 unloaded boxes and 0.4\%
of the ULDs out of range, against 729, 24 and 10.1\%); with $\lambda = 0.5$ the algorithm
uses 699 ULDs and leaves 20 boxes unloaded with 2.7\% of the ULDs out of range, and with
$\lambda = 0$ it uses 679 ULDs and leaves 19 boxes unloaded, but half of the ULDs violate the
CoG constraint.
