\section{Computational experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

This section evaluates the proposed algorithm on the benchmark of
\citet{Paquay2018b} and on the two adapted variants introduced by \citet{Hessler2025}, and
compares it instance by instance with the randomized greedy search (RGS) of
\citet{Hessler2025}, which, to the best of our knowledge, reports the best results on these
instances. Section~\ref{sec:exp-setup} describes the instances, the implementation, the
parameter tuning and the evaluation protocol. Section~\ref{sec:exp-results} reports the main
results, Section~\ref{sec:exp-bound} compares them with a lower bound,
Section~\ref{sec:exp-time} analyzes the running time, Section~\ref{sec:exp-ablation} studies
the contribution of each component and Section~\ref{sec:exp-tradeoff} the trade-off between the
number of ULDs and the weight distribution.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

\paragraph{Instances.}
We use the 300 final instances of \citet{Paquay2018b}: ten groups of 30 instances with
$|I| \in \{10, 20, \dots, 100\}$ boxes sampled from a real set of 562 boxes, highly heterogeneous,
with weights, allowed vertical orientations and fragile (non-stackable) boxes. Six ULD types
of a Boeing 777 are available in unlimited number: three lower-deck containers (types 1--3,
with up to two bottom corners cut to fit the fuselage) and three main-deck pallets and
containers (types 4--6, with up to two top corners cut). Each ULD type has a weight capacity and
a maximum deviation of the center of gravity (CoG) from its geometric center in the length and
width directions, and a maximum height of the CoG \citep{Paquay2016}.
Following \citet{Hessler2025}, we consider three settings:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Paquay}: the original instances. A box is supported if its four bottom corners
	rest on non-fragile boxes or on the floor, and there is no padding material. For these
	instances, \citet{Hessler2025} set the maximum CoG deviation to 5\% of the half length and
	half width of the ULD, which is stricter than the deviations of the instance file (10--20\% of
	the half dimensions); we use the same limit.
\item \textbf{Adapted}: the ULD floor has an edge of width $e = 10$\,mm for the cargo net locks,
	on which no box can be loaded below the height $\delta = 10$\,mm; a substructure of height
	$\delta$ can be used to raise the floor. A box is supported if its four bottom corners are
	supported or if part of its base is directly supported and at least 90\% of its base is
	supported directly or by padding material of height at most 10\,mm. The allowed CoG
	deviation is 10\% of the half length and half width of the ULD. These are the default
	problem parameters of \citet[Table~2]{Hessler2025}.
\item \textbf{One}: the adapted instances with only one ULD of each type, so that some boxes may
	remain unloaded. As in \citet{Hessler2025}, solutions are compared lexicographically: first
	the loaded volume, then the number of ULDs.
\end{itemize}
In the three settings the objective is the total volume of the ULDs \citep{Paquay2016,Hessler2025}
(in the setting \emph{One}, after the loaded volume); we also report the number of ULDs. Our
algorithm uses the same parameters and components in the three settings; only the problem data
(edge, support criterion, CoG limits and number of ULDs) change.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
The algorithm is implemented in C++ (compiled with g++ 13.3, \texttt{-O3}). Each run uses a
single thread; the experiments were carried out on a server with two Intel Xeon E5-2620 v4
processors (8 cores each, 2.1\,GHz, 3.0\,GHz in turbo mode, hyper-threading disabled) and
62\,GB of RAM, running 14 instances in parallel.
According to the single-thread rating of PassMark \citep{PassMark}, one core of the Apple M2 Pro
used by \citet{Hessler2025} is about 2.5 times faster than one core of our processor (4105
against 1624).
The time limit $t$ is the time of each run of BSG in the construction, excluding the generation
of the blocks; in the improvement phases (\textsc{Insert} and the reduction), which load many ULDs
again, each run of BSG is limited to $0.1\,t$ (Section~\ref{sec:exp-time} compares it with
$t$). We report results for $t = 0.1$\,s and $t = 1$\,s. Since a ULD can be loaded several times (with
and without the substructure and in the improvement phases), the total
time per instance is several times larger, and we report it separately. The code, the instances, the solutions and the scripts that
produce all the tables are available at \url{https://github.com/rilianx/bsg-uld}.

\paragraph{Parameter tuning.}
The parameters of the algorithm were tuned once with irace \citep{LopezIbanez2016}, as
\citet{Paquay2018b} did for their heuristic, on a separate training set, and are used unchanged
in the three settings and for both time limits. The training set consists of 150 preliminary
instances of \citet{Paquay2018b} (30 instances with 30, 40, 50, 60 and 100 boxes), disjoint
from the final ones, each one in the three settings (450 runs, presented to irace in blocks of
the three settings of an instance), with $t = 0.1$\,s, $N_{\max} = 500$ blocks (fixed, to bound the time per ULD) and a budget of
6000 runs; no
configuration is discarded before 21 instances. The cost of a run is
\[
\frac{V_{\mathrm{ULD}} + 100\, V_{\mathrm{unl}} + 150\,\mathrm{m}^3 \cdot n_{\mathrm{CoG}}}{V_{\mathrm{boxes}}},
\]
where $V_{\mathrm{ULD}}$ is the volume of the ULDs, $V_{\mathrm{unl}}$ the volume left unloaded,
$n_{\mathrm{CoG}}$ the number of ULDs whose CoG is out of range and $V_{\mathrm{boxes}}$ the
volume of the boxes of the instance, so that loading all the boxes comes first, then the
CoG and then the volume of the ULDs. The values of a preliminary tuning were the initial
configuration. Table~\ref{tab:params} lists the parameters; the four elite
configurations returned by irace agree on a small $\lambda$ (1.0--1.7), a large $\gamma$
(0.77--0.97) and $\theta$ between 0.18 and 0.32.

\paragraph{Validation and comparison.}
Every solution is written to a file (position and orientation of each box) and checked by an
independent validator, which does not share code with the solver: each box is loaded at most
once, in an allowed orientation, inside its ULD and its cuts, outside the floor edge below
$\delta$, without overlapping other boxes, supported according to the setting and not on top
of a fragile box, and the weight capacity of each ULD is respected. The validator also counts
the ULDs whose CoG is out of the allowed deviation in the length or width direction (CoG$_{xy}$)
and above the maximum height (CoG$_z$, a constraint of \citet{Paquay2016} that
\citet{Hessler2025} do not consider). All the 9000 solutions reported in this section are
feasible.
The comparison with \citet{Hessler2025} uses their instance-by-instance results (Tables 8, 10
and 12 of their online appendix). For each instance we count whether our solution is better (smaller
total volume of the ULDs or, in the setting \emph{One}, more loaded volume or, with the same
loaded volume, smaller total volume of the ULDs), equal or worse, and we test the difference in
the total volume of the ULDs with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The total volume of the ULDs of
the RGS is computed from the type of each ULD (Tables 9, 11 and 13 of their appendix) and, in the
setting \emph{One}, its unloaded volume from the utilization of each ULD, which is rounded to
0.1\%; we therefore consider two loaded volumes equal if they differ by less than 0.05\,m$^3$.
Each configuration is run five times; we report the mean and the standard deviation.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:exp-results}

Table~\ref{tab:results} summarizes the results. In the three settings, our algorithm uses less
ULD volume than the RGS of \citet{Hessler2025}: 4.5\% ($t = 0.1$\,s) and
7.2\% ($t = 1$\,s) less in the Paquay setting, 3.6\% and 6.4\% less in the
adapted one, and 4.8\% and 8.0\% less with one ULD of each type, where it also leaves less than
half of the volume unloaded. Instance by instance, its solutions are better in 178.2 instances
and worse in 60.8 in the Paquay setting with $t = 1$\,s (168.6 and 62.2 in the adapted one,
176.4 and 27 with one ULD of each type), and the differences in volume are significant in all
the cases (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, $p \le 3 \cdot 10^{-4}$ with $t = 0.1$\,s and
$p < 10^{-7}$ with $t = 1$\,s). The total cost of the ULDs given in the instance files decreases
in the same way (7.7\%, 7.0\% and 8.2\% with $t = 1$\,s). At most 1.4\% of our ULDs violate the
CoG constraint in the length or width direction, whereas 7--11\% of the ULDs of the RGS have the
CoG out of range.

Since the objective is the volume, our algorithm replaces large ULDs by smaller ones whenever
this saves volume, even if it uses more ULDs. With $t = 1$\,s it still uses fewer ULDs than the RGS in
the three settings (695.2, 639.0 and 709.4 against 708, 653 and 729, with more boxes loaded in
the last one); with $t = 0.1$\,s it uses 1.7--4.3\% more ULDs. In the setting with one ULD of each type, it leaves 9 boxes (9.0\,m$^3$)
unloaded with $t = 1$\,s and 12 boxes (10.4\,m$^3$) with $t = 0.1$\,s, against 24 boxes
(25.9\,m$^3$) for the RGS.

The results are stable: the total volume of the five runs of each configuration varies by less
than 0.2\%. The running time is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:exp-time}.

\subsection{Comparison with a lower bound}
\label{sec:exp-bound}

A lower bound on the total volume of the ULDs is the smallest total volume of a set of ULDs
whose volume and weight capacity suffice for the boxes and in which every box fits in some ULD,
computed with the CP-SAT solver of OR-Tools. It assumes that the ULDs can be completely filled,
which is far from possible with these boxes: in the Paquay setting the bound is 10\,141\,m$^3$,
against 17\,309\,m$^3$ for our solutions with $t = 1$\,s, whose ULDs are about 60\% full. A
simple bound on the number of ULDs (the total volume of the boxes divided by the volume of the
largest ULD) is reached by our solutions in 106 of the 300 instances with $t = 1$\,s, against
80 for the RGS, although the number of ULDs is only a secondary measure here.

\subsection{Running time}
\label{sec:exp-time}

With $t = 1$\,s our algorithm takes 28.1--44.0\,s per instance on average (at most 302\,s),
and with $t = 0.1$\,s 3.7--5.8\,s (at most 36\,s), against 0.2--0.4\,s for the RGS on an Apple
M2 Pro, which is about 2.5 times faster than one core of our processor according to the
single-thread rating of PassMark \citep{PassMark}. Most of the time is spent in the reduction
phase, which loads many ULDs again. Table~\ref{tab:time} shows how the results change with the
time limit in the Paquay setting. With $t = 0.02$\,s the mean time per instance is 1.0\,s,
about 0.4\,s on the processor of \citet{Hessler2025}, comparable to the RGS, and the volume is
0.5\% smaller than that of the RGS (not significant); from $t = 0.05$\,s on, the difference is
significant and it grows with the time limit up to 7.5\% with $t = 2$\,s. Figure~\ref{fig:time} shows the same curve
against the mean time per instance, with the time of the RGS on its machine and scaled to ours.

\paragraph{Weight distribution.}
Without the penalty $\lambda$ the CoG is out of range in 285--446 ULDs; the volume of the ULDs
changes little (it decreases in the Paquay setting, where the CoG limit of 5\% is very strict,
and increases in the other two). Without the translation of the load the number of violations
also grows to 119--178 ULDs and the volume increases, since the penalty then rejects loadings
that a translation would have balanced. Penalizing the CoG excess in the evaluation of the
blocks ($\mu = 0.5$) does not reduce the number of violations consistently and its effect on the
volume changes sign from one setting to another; we therefore do not use it. Anchoring the
spaces at the nearest corner instead of the origin, with a grouped reload of the ULDs whose CoG
is out of range, gives a similar volume at a higher time, and the anchoring at the origin is
simpler.

\paragraph{Reduction phase.}
The reduction phase is the component that makes the difference with the RGS: without it, the
volume is about the same as that of the RGS in the Paquay and adapted settings, and the
elimination of ULDs alone, which reduces the number of ULDs, recovers only a small part of the
difference. Allowing the boxes left over to be loaded into two new ULDs instead of one reduces
the volume by a further 0.4--0.6 points. The three stages of \textsc{Insert} contribute between
0.5 and 1.3 points each; the reload from scratch matters most for the unloaded volume when the
number of ULDs is limited.

\paragraph{Choosing the type of each ULD and restarts.}
Choosing the type of each ULD with $\theta$ instead of the rule of \citet{Hessler2025} alone
reduces the volume by about one point in the Paquay setting and with one ULD of each type, and
has a small effect in the adapted one: with an unlimited number of ULDs a poor choice during
the construction is mostly corrected later by the reduction phase, but with one ULD of each type
the reduction has few alternatives. In an earlier version without the reduction phase, the same
rule, as well as evaluating each candidate type by completing the solution with a fast
construction (a rollout), reduced the volume by less than 1.5\% in the Paquay setting: local
choices do not anticipate which boxes remain for the next ULDs, which the reduction phase
handles. The restarts of the construction halve the unloaded volume with one ULD of each type
(10.4 against 19.8\,m$^3$), at the price of a slightly larger volume of the ULDs, since the
boxes that are loaded need space.

\subsection{Volume of the ULDs versus weight distribution}
\label{sec:exp-tradeoff}
